Honest scope: these are solar events (CMEs, flares) recorded by Sun-Earth-line spacecraft (SOHO, STEREO, GOES) via NASA's DONKI catalog. They are not measurements of radiation at Mars. This project uses published RAD measurements; DONKI is solar context, not a current local Mars dose. Solar particle events take hours to days to reach Mars, and Mars is usually far from the Sun-Earth line. Treat this panel as "what is the Sun doing", not "is it safe to go outside".

What we actually measured on Mars

  • MSL Curiosity's RAD instrument (Gale Crater, 2012-2013): average dose equivalent on the surface 0.64 ± 0.12 mSv/day (absorbed dose 0.21 ± 0.04 mGy/day) - roughly half the dose measured during the cruise to Mars, thanks to shielding by the atmosphere and the planet itself.
  • That is about 230 mSv per Earth year on the surface, before any habitat shielding - the central number for crew career-dose planning at any of the four sites.
  • One solar energetic particle (SEP) event added about 50 µSv in a day on top of the background - solar storms matter, but the everyday galactic cosmic ray background dominates.
  • Site effect: deeper sites (Gale -4.4 km, Arcadia -3 to -4 km, Valles floor -4 to -5 km) sit under more atmosphere = more shielding; Jezero at -2.6 km gets somewhat less.

Dust and other environmental hazards

  • Mars has regional and occasional planet-encircling dust storms (the 2018 global storm ended the Opportunity rover). They cut solar power and visibility for weeks - any site plan needs stored power or nuclear backup.
  • Fine dust is present at all four sites; NASA's ice map marks some dusty ground as unsafe to land on (it may not bear a lander's weight).

Sources: Hassler et al. 2014, Science 343 (MSL RAD); Zeitlin et al. 2013; NASA DONKI (CCMC); NASA/JPL dust-storm coverage.

AI (SI) use: an AI assistant proposed four candidate Mars landing sites; the human contributor chose to combine all four into one concept (“a Mars map where astronauts can compare four places and pick the safest one with water”) and directed the build. The AI implemented everything else (code, design, slides, text). Published data (map layers, dose rate, elevations, ice evidence) come from the sources above. The 0-100 Crew Safety Index scores and weights are the AI assistant's own heuristic choices from that evidence - not published measurements, not a validated safety model, and not astronaut safety guidance.
